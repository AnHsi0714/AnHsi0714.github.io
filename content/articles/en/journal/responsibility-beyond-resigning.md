## Backstory

This piece grew out of my midterm reflection for the Interpersonal Relationships and Communication course.

After watching the first few episodes, I wanted to look into why Brian was fired at the end of season 3, episode 2 of The Apprentice[^1].

The task was to renovate a motel with a $20,000 budget and let paying guests rate it. Brian, the project manager, spent nearly the whole budget on bathroom fixtures and similar upgrades, yet his team's rating still came in below the rival team's (Net Worth, the street-smarts team, 2.92; Magna, the book-smarts team, about 3.96 to 3.97), which sent them to the boardroom.

At first, I thought Brian bravely took responsibility. He told Trump that the biggest problem in the task was himself. I expected him to be appreciated for it, but Trump and his advisors reacted with surprise and disdain.

The format of this episode was also unusual. By the show's convention, the project manager picks two teammates to go with them into the final boardroom, where Trump chooses who to fire among the three. In this episode nobody went into the final boardroom, and Brian was fired on the spot, with no three-person elimination round.

My first guess was that the show stresses competition, so contestants have to avoid elimination at all costs, even by getting rivals knocked out. But after talking it through with an AI, I started to see that Brian's problem wasn't that he was willing to take responsibility. It was that both he and I had misunderstood what real responsibility is.

## Responsibility is more than admitting fault

On the surface, Brian owned up to the failure and even offered to fire himself. But he didn't show that he understood why the task failed, and he didn't offer an improvement plan that could convince Trump. To a boss, admitting fault is only the starting point. What matters more is whether the employee can learn from the failure and prove they can still solve the problem.

This made me rethink what taking responsibility means. I used to believe that after making a mistake, apologizing and admitting it was enough. After this episode, I understood that **taking responsibility is not just admitting fault or accepting punishment. It also means facing the consequences of the mistake, understanding why it happened, and taking action to improve.**

**Brian's problem wasn't an unwillingness to take responsibility. He stopped at admitting fault and accepting punishment, without showing he could fix the problem.**

## From business to political responsibility

Extending this from business to politics raises more questions. We often say politicians should step down to take responsibility and pay for bad policy. But there is another view: they should clean up their own mess before they leave.

When a leader can't handle a failure well, should they stay and get a chance to improve, or should someone better suited take over? And even if they step down, how do we make sure they still carry the cleanup responsibility they owe?

In Taiwan, it is not unusual for officials to step down after a policy or political situation causes controversy. For example, Chen Chi-chung resigned as Minister of Agriculture in 2023 after the controversy over the egg import program. On the night of the 2022 local elections, Su Tseng-chang offered to resign but was asked to stay, and the cabinet did not resign en masse until January 2023.

There may be several considerations behind this:

1. **A change in policy direction**: the leader's expertise or approach no longer fits the new policy needs.
2. **Lower political trust**: under public pressure or lost political support, the organization may choose to replace the leader.
3. **Reassignment**: the person leaves the post and someone else takes over the work, but they may still take part in handling the aftermath.

Leaving a position doesn't mean you can't keep handling the aftermath, though. In 2024, Huang Shan-shan was suspended for three years over the Taiwan People's Party's political donation accounting dispute, yet she continued to lead the cleanup team's audit of the accounts. Some inside the party questioned whether a suspended member leading the audit made her both player and referee, but this piece doesn't go further into that question.

All three paths end with the person leaving their post, but the actual effects differ. Perhaps responsibility can be broken down more finely.

## Is responsibility only about success or failure?

I think the political examples show that responsibility isn't just whether a project succeeded or failed. Behind it there are three distinct aspects:

1. **Accountability**: whether someone should explain a decision and its consequences, accept scrutiny, and bear the corresponding consequences.
2. **Fitness for the role**: whether the person is still capable of performing the job, and whether the organization should keep giving them resources and power.
3. **Project responsibility**: whether the person in charge carried out the task toward the goal, managed resources well, and dealt properly with missed targets. This is what the public usually thinks of as project responsibility.

**These three aren't mutually exclusive. They can all exist in the same event.** For example, a leader may need to be held accountable but still be capable of handling the follow-up work. Or a leader may step down because they're no longer fit for the role, yet still have to cooperate with investigations and cleanup.

In other words, what responsibility requires can differ depending on the kind of project. In business there may be room to make amends, with firing or compensation as the worst case. In politics, stepping down is often the most visible form of accountability, and it can also be shaped by public opinion and party interests. But **stepping down does not mean the problem is solved, and it certainly doesn't mean the person has finished everything they owe.**

## Takeaway

So I think whether someone is responsible can't be judged only by whether they stepped down, or only by whether they admitted fault. What matters is **whether they are willing to face the problem, bear the consequences they should, and take real action to deal with it.**

Stepping down can be one way of taking responsibility, but it doesn't mean the responsibility is complete. Even after leaving the post, the person may still need to explain how the decision was made, cooperate with investigations, or help with remediation. What deserves attention is whether they fulfill their obligations and whether the problem is actually handled well.
