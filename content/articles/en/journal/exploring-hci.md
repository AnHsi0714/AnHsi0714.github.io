> Written between 2026-07-25 and 2026-08-08

This is a piece recording my journey into HCI and what I've taken away from it so far.

## Origins

I've been in the CS department for a bit over three years now, and if I count the three years before that in the vocational information-tech track, it's been more than six years. Through all of it, I've worked across hardware, systems, and software — electronics and soldering in the vocational track, operating systems and computer organization in the CS degree — but none of these areas ever managed to spark real passion in me. Eventually I realized that what I actually get invested in was never building out a system's functionality for its own sake, but rather the pull to ask "how will people understand and use this thing?" It wasn't until I recently started preparing my graduate school application materials that I stumbled onto the field of HCI — and realized the thing I'd been drawn to all along already had a name.

What I actually enjoy is exploring the interaction between people and technology: whether it was, back when I first entered the information-tech track, simply wanting to build a game idea I'd cooked up with a middle-school classmate; or discovering, during the summer after my first year of college, Professor Wu Che-Yu's online courses on front-end web design and generative art, which showed me the side of technology that meets the humanities; or, in everyday software use, catching myself noticing how interface design shapes the experience, and feeling that small pang of regret whenever I see an odd color scheme or a poorly designed interface. All of these moments led me to realize that there was, in fact, a corner of computer science that matched my interests all along.

## An Unexpected Discovery

Looking back, it's actually hard to pin down exactly when I started getting interested in HCI. At the time I didn't even know the field "human-computer interaction" existed, and I wasn't deliberately steering toward any research direction — I'd just find myself reacting, on instinct, to certain interface designs: why does this button just look clickable? Why do some websites, despite packing in plenty of features, feel so clunky to use? Why does the same piece of information, presented in different ways, end up being so much easier or harder to understand?

Back then I'd usually just file those reactions under "good design vs. bad design," without realizing these questions could actually be broken down, observed, even tested through experiments. It wasn't until I started putting together my graduate school application materials that I seriously went looking for a field that could describe these questions — and that's how I found HCI.

HCI stands for Human-Computer Interaction. At first I was wondering how I'd even find a project to get into this field — only to realize my capstone project had already brushed up against it: CodePulse, a data structures and algorithms visualization learning platform. How I designed that study and what I found is written up in a separate piece: [A User Study, Seen Through CodePulse](/articles/code-pulse-user-study). It was also the first time I actually tried using research methods, instead of just judging whether an interaction design worked on intuition alone.

These experiences showed me that my past judgments of whether an interaction design "worked" had mostly been operating on intuition alone. I started wanting to learn more about experimental design, statistical analysis, and how research questions get formed — methods that could turn that intuition into research questions I could actually validate and discuss.

User feedback was the first time I noticed that "intuitive" isn't just a simple subjective adjective. When I started asking myself why some interactions get understood naturally while others need extra explanation, that's what led me further into HCI concepts like affordance, signifiers, feedback, and mapping — which I wrote up as a separate piece: [Learning "Good Design" Through CodePulse](/articles/code-pulse-good-design).

That's also what got me trying to look back at interfaces I'd already built through an HCI lens. I used to just say things like "this button looks pretty intuitive" or "this screen has too much information," but now I've started asking: why does it feel intuitive? And what exactly makes information hard to take in?

So I started getting to know HCI from the most basic question of all: what actually makes a design good?
