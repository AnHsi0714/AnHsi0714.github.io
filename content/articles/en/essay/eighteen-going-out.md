"Eighteen Years Old and Setting Out" is a short story Yu Hua published in 1987, one of the defining works of his early avant-garde style and the first piece to draw real attention to him as a writer. What's read here is an excerpted passage. It follows an eighteen-year-old boy on his first solo trip away from home, who runs into a string of disjointed, even violent encounters: turned away by drivers, robbed of a bag of apples, betrayed by someone he thought was a friend, all of it a metaphor for the shock and disillusionment of a young person's first real confrontation with the world.

## Reading Notes

Finishing this piece, the feeling that stayed with me most was how cruel reality can be. It opens with the protagonist spotting a truck on the road; he waves it down, but it drives straight past without even a glance back, and for a moment he assumes there'll be plenty more chances like this one. He soon learns otherwise: let one chance slip, and it's a long walk before the next one comes along.

"The highway rose and fell, and each rise kept tempting me, tempting me to race up it and see the inn, but every time all I found was another rise, with a discouraging dip in between." This line drives the point home: the road to any destination is never straight or smooth, but full of rises and falls. Every time you reach what looks like the end, it turns out to be the start of the next stretch: the terrain doesn't just measure how hard the goal is to reach, it tests a person's persistence along the way.

The next time he runs into a vehicle, the scene shifts: offering the driver a cigarette gets him nowhere, and whatever social rules or codes of courtesy he assumed would apply simply don't. Chastened by missing the first ride, he resorts to something blunter: just yanking the door open and climbing in. Once inside, caught up in conversation and the scenery outside, he stops thinking about the inn altogether, only remembering his actual goal (finding somewhere safe to rest) once the truck breaks down. It's a neat illustration of how easily people forget what they set out to do the moment something pleasant distracts them. Then come the bicycle bandits and the theft of his bag, and the person he'd taken for a friend turns out to be one of them: another reminder, delivered hard, of just how unsentimental the world can be.

By the end, the protagonist is battered and bruised all over, and the truck, the one thing still carrying a trace of those earlier good moments, has been stripped down just as badly. The inn he'd been searching for the whole time turns out to have been the truck itself, all along, not unlike the story of the Blue Bird. The only thing still there for him, in the end, is the truck, stuck in the very same situation he is. And the story closes on that image, set against the hopeful, idyllic opening, which only makes the contrast sharper.

To me, this is a story about the growth and change a person goes through after being tested by the world: its insistence on reality keeps cutting against what the title first suggests, which sounds more like a leisurely travelogue abroad. That the protagonist still comes out "warm" after everything that happens to him is itself a vivid example, a reminder that no matter what challenges we face, we should keep pushing forward toward what we set out to do.
