"Father's Seven Days" is Liu Zijie's essay recording her father's funeral, winner of the Lin Rong-san Literary Award for prose and later adapted into a film of the same name. The piece runs along the timeline of traditional funeral rites, from the first seven-day mourning period to the burial, tracing how the author comes to realize, piece by piece over those seven days, that her father is truly gone.

## Reading Notes

Finishing this piece left me with a heavy feeling. The nurse pumping the ventilator bag in a steady rhythm at the very end reads as absurd and yet unmistakably real: the author has to face her father's death honestly, without looking away, and still it hurts. By the third day, she starts replaying their last meeting, his last words, and the ordinary moments of the past resurface one by one as memories of the present. "You only start to cherish something after you've lost it" fits perfectly here. The funeral customs described might be handled differently by today's standards, but it's precisely that tradition which manages to comfort a grieving family.

The scrambling to find things on the seventh day at the cremation, the return to normal life on the eighth: wanting to move on but still catching yourself remembering. Several places get suggested, but in the end the author chooses to go "light." And yet when she buys a lottery ticket, she still wants him included in it (or maybe that's just another word for remembering him), the same way that, boarding a plane at the very end, she wants to buy a pack of Long Life cigarettes for a relative who's already gone. The mind keeps drifting back and forth between forgetting and missing someone, always looking for some trace that's still there.

Departed relatives, past family bonds, and a great deal of Taiwanese dialect and custom recur throughout the piece. Tradition might not suit modern life anymore (it changes, gets abandoned), but for someone who has passed on, it's still the custom that fits them best. Losing her father at 53 leaves the author unable to let go, but given the scars left by all those tubes, maybe leaving really was the better choice. Maybe it wasn't a choice at all, but either way, it came full circle, as if echoing the line from the text: "Now your body is whole again, no wounds, no scars, no sickness, no suffering, just like when you were young and setting out to make your way." Back to that first, good impression: a time that has already become a memory.
