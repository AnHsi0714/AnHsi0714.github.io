## Why "reflexive" specifically

In qualitative research, "Thematic Analysis" isn't a single method. The more traditional, common approach is the coding reliability tradition: multiple independent researchers each code the same data, then a coefficient like Cohen's Kappa measures how much their codes overlap. High overlap is what counts as "objective enough, trustworthy."

Reflexive Thematic Analysis, proposed by Braun and Clarke, starts from a completely different premise: <span data-term="thematic-coding">codes and themes</span> are meaning the researcher subjectively constructs by interpreting the data, not an objective answer that was already hiding in the data waiting to be "discovered." A different team analyzing the same dataset arriving at different codes and themes is normal, even expected, because every researcher brings different background knowledge and points of focus into the analysis.

## So what is team discussion actually for

If the coding is expected to differ from researcher to researcher anyway, what's the point of the team discussing it together in a reflexive approach? The answer: the discussion isn't there to "calibrate" toward one answer everyone agrees on. It's there to push the researcher to articulate why they interpreted something the way they did, and to check whether other readings got overlooked, so the interpretation itself becomes more solid and more self-aware. That's what "reflexive" actually refers to.

## How this was used in the SnapPI study

The original study behind the SnapPI reading note used exactly this approach: the first author coded 21 participants' interview transcripts and returned Snap screenshots first, tagging meaning units like "quantifying feelings that are hard to put into words." The research team then met regularly to revise and merge related codes, eventually converging on 9 codes and 3 core themes. Throughout that process, the team discussion's role was to fill in blind spots and round out the first author's interpretation, not to compute agreement scores against other coders' results.

## Which approach to pick

Neither approach is "more rigorous." They just serve different goals. If what a study cares about is whether a coding scheme still holds up when applied to new data, i.e. reproducibility, the coding reliability tradition is the better fit. If what it cares about is getting as close as possible to the data itself and understanding what participants actually meant, i.e. depth of interpretation, the reflexive approach fits better.
